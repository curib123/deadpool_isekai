# Chapter 1 — Wrong Forest, Wrong World

**Status:** CANON — VOICE REVISION UNDER GATE 9 REVIEW  
**Revision Scope:** Voice/comedy/fourth-wall only; story canon unchanged

The first thing I noticed was that I was breathing.

That may sound like a very low standard for a successful morning, but the last thing I remembered was rain exploding across a windshield, white headlights where they absolutely should not have been, and several tons of metal making a persuasive argument against my continued existence.

So yes.

Breathing was encouraging.

Before you ask, no, I did not immediately sit up and announce that I had been reincarnated in another world. I had standards. Very flexible standards, apparently, but standards.

I kept my eyes closed for another second and took inventory. Head attached. Arms attached. Legs still employed by the organization. Nothing felt crushed, and there was no glass in my face.

No screaming either. No sirens. No paramedic leaning over me and saying something medically responsible like, “Sir, please stop trying to move.”

Instead, I could hear leaves shifting overhead.

I opened my eyes.

Green filled my vision. Not hospital green. Not traffic-light green. Actual leaves layered so thick above me that the sky only appeared in thin patches. Damp air pressed against my skin, and something unfamiliar called somewhere beyond the trees.

I stared upward for a few seconds.

“Okay.”

My voice worked.

Good. Strong start.

For the record, I would have accepted a hospital. I would even have accepted one of those suspiciously clean white rooms where somebody explains that your previous life has ended and offers you three broken abilities as compensation.

There was no white room.

No goddess.

No floating screen.

No deep mysterious voice saying, Welcome, chosen one.

Nothing.

Just me, wet soil, trees I did not recognize, and what I sincerely hoped was not poisonous vegetation under my right hand.

You would think reality could provide onboarding.

I pushed myself upright. The movement felt wrong.

Not painful.

Wrong.

My balance shifted faster than expected, and I caught myself before I tipped forward. My arms looked like my arms, mostly. Same general proportions. Same hands. But there was more strength in the movement than memory said there should be, and my shoulders felt strangely light.

Even my breathing settled too quickly.

I flexed my fingers, then my toes. Because dignity had already left the premises, I patted down my ribs, stomach, neck, face, and the back of my head.

Nothing broken. No blood. No obvious hole where an important organ had resigned.

The clothes were strange too: dark fabric, fitted but flexible, nothing I remembered putting on before the crash.

No wallet.

No keys.

No phone.

I checked again for the phone.

Still absent.

“Excellent.”

That one was for me, not you. You get the polished version. I get the private moment where I quietly consider whether I am dead, insane, kidnapped, drugged, or starring in a genre famous for terrible workplace safety.

I stood.

The forest did not applaud, but I straightened anyway. If I was going to wake up alone in an impossible place, I could at least do it with posture.

A cold drop of water fell from a leaf and landed directly on the back of my neck.

I flinched hard enough to nearly trip over a root.

The forest remained unimpressed.

Fine.

We were establishing boundaries.

I looked around properly. The trunks were tall and dark with moisture. Fern-like plants crowded the lower ground beneath broad silver-veined leaves I had never seen before. Moss climbed exposed stones in thick layers, and the earth smelled rich, cold, and aggressively real.

No tire noise.

No aircraft.

No power lines.

No distant city hum.

I turned slowly and listened.

Nothing human answered.

That was when the joke stopped being quite as funny.

The crash came back in pieces. Rain. Brakes. Light. Impact.

Then this.

I rubbed a hand over my face and forced myself to breathe slowly. Dreams did not usually come with cold mud soaking through your knee, and hallucinations probably did not bother rendering this much texture.

I pinched the inside of my arm.

“Ow.”

Useful experiment. I had officially proven that I was capable of hurting myself in mysterious forests.

Science would be proud.

You probably want me to tell you that this was the moment I accepted the obvious.

Another world.

Reincarnation.

Isekai.

Pick your label.

I did not.

Saying ridiculous things confidently is how they become your problem.

Instead, I chose a direction where the ground rose slightly and started walking. My survival strategy was extremely advanced:

Do not die.

Find people.

Ask questions.

You may notice there was no fourth step.

I believe in flexible planning.

The terrain made fun of that confidence almost immediately. Roots twisted beneath wet leaves, thorny vines grabbed at my clothes, and something small and winged buzzed beside my ear for half a minute with the persistence of a debt collector.

I climbed the rise, slid halfway back down, caught a branch, and pretended the entire sequence had been intentional.

There are moments when a man becomes one with nature.

This was not one of them.

At the top, the trees thinned enough for me to see farther ahead. I did not find rooftops or smoke, but I found something better than random wilderness.

Stone.

Not a natural outcrop. A line of old, half-buried stones cut across the slope beneath moss and roots. I crouched beside them and brushed dirt away, revealing edges too straight to be accidental.

A drainage channel, maybe.

Or the remains of a road.

I followed it.

The farther I went, the clearer it became that people had once been here. A rotten timber lay parallel to the stones. A low block stood at one side of the route, weathered almost smooth. Brush grew differently in a long strip where the ground had once been kept clear.

Civilization.

Old civilization. Abandoned civilization. Possibly civilization that had been eaten by something unpleasant.

Still counting it as progress.

Then I saw the tracks.

Four impressions sat in damp soil ahead of me, large enough that I stopped walking. I crouched again.

Four toes, maybe. Claws. The print was wider than my palm, with several more leading into the brush.

Fresh ones.

I looked into the trees.

“Of course.”

This time, I said it quietly.

Something moved in the brush behind me.

I turned.

Nothing.

A few leaves trembled.

I kept walking, but not faster. Faster would look like panic, and I was not panicking.

I was strategically increasing my distance from the unknown predator while maintaining an atmosphere of calm authority.

There was another rustle.

Closer.

My pace increased.

Still not panic.

Then a low shape crossed between two trees ahead of me.

I stopped.

It was about the size of a large dog, maybe slightly bigger, but too lean through the middle. Its front legs were long. Its shoulders rolled beneath dark coarse fur. Its head sat lower than I liked, narrow muzzle pointed directly at me.

I did not know what it was.

It knew exactly what I was.

Lunch.

“Listen,” I told it, because apparently negotiation with wildlife was where my life had gone. “I am having a very complicated day.”

The animal stared.

I lifted my chin and gave it the look.

You know the one.

The look that says, I have killed things far more dangerous than you.

Technically, I had killed mosquitoes.

The animal bared its teeth.

No respect for theater.

Disappointing.

It moved first.

Fast.

I jumped sideways and learned two important facts at once.

One: this body was quicker than expected.

Two: quicker was not the same as skilled.

My foot hit loose mud. I went down on one knee as teeth snapped where my thigh had been a moment earlier.

I grabbed the nearest branch and swung.

It broke across the animal's shoulder.

The animal did not appear emotionally affected.

“Right.”

I scrambled backward while it circled. I kept the broken branch between us, though half a stick is only intimidating when the other side understands symbolism.

It lunged again.

I jammed the branch forward.

The animal twisted around it and slammed into me.

Pain ripped across my left forearm.

Hot.

Immediate.

Real.

I shouted and hit the ground hard enough to lose my breath. For half a second, I just stared at my arm.

Blood.

Four ragged cuts ran from below my elbow toward my wrist. Not deep enough to expose anything I never wanted to see, but deep enough that blood was already running over my hand.

The animal came around again.

That was when the fear arrived properly.

No joke.

No clever observation for you.

No private performance for me.

I was alone, unarmed, bleeding, and something with teeth had already learned that I was not very good at this.

I got up badly and backed toward the remains of the roadside wall. The animal lowered itself, watching.

No weapon.

No room.

I looked for a rock.

Nothing big enough.

A board.

A pole.

A shield.

Anything.

The animal sprang.

I threw my injured arm up and had one stupid, desperate thought.

I need something between us.

Something appeared.

Not elegantly.

There was no flash of divine light. No satisfying sound. No legendary weapon spinning into my hand because destiny had finally remembered its cue.

One moment there was empty air.

The next there was a thick, ugly slab of dark material half across my forearm, like someone had tried to build a shield from a bad memory of a door.

The animal hit it.

The impact drove me backward into the stones. My shoulder screamed, but the teeth did not reach me.

For one deeply confusing second, the animal and I both seemed surprised.

I reacted first.

I shoved.

The slab was heavier than it looked.

Or lighter.

Somehow both, depending on the angle.

I nearly lost it, recovered, and swung the edge into the animal's muzzle. It yelped and stumbled.

I swung again, but the second strike barely connected because the thing in my hands had already started losing shape.

The surface rippled. One corner vanished. The whole object felt soft and wrong.

The animal backed away.

I raised what remained of the slab.

“Exactly,” I said, breathing hard. “Calculated.”

It growled.

Please do not insult either of us by believing me.

The animal hesitated, apparently decided I had become too strange to eat efficiently, and disappeared into the brush.

I waited.

Five seconds.

Ten.

Long enough for the forest to settle again.

The object in my hands sagged like melting wax without the heat, thinned into nothing, and left me gripping empty air.

I stared at my hands.

Then at the space where it had been.

Then back at my hands.

“No.”

I looked around for it.

Gone.

I pictured the slab again.

Nothing.

I tried harder.

Still nothing.

“Good,” I whispered. “That would have been too convenient.”

My arm throbbed.

Right.

Actual problem.

I sat against the broken roadside stones and inspected the wound. The cuts were ugly.

Less ugly than they had been.

I frowned.

Blood still coated my skin, but the flow had slowed almost completely. The torn edges looked tighter.

I wiped carefully with the least filthy part of my sleeve and watched the deepest cut narrow.

Not metaphorically.

A hot crawling ache moved beneath the skin, sharp enough to make my fingers shake.

The wound kept changing.

Slowly.

Painfully.

Impossible.

“Nope.”

I looked away.

Then back.

Still happening.

I pressed two fingers beside the cut.

Bad idea.

Pain shot up my arm. Whatever this was, it had not come with the kindness package.

I leaned my head against the stone and closed my eyes.

All right.

You and I should probably review.

I remembered dying.

Or almost dying. Details pending.

I woke up in a forest that did not belong anywhere I recognized. Something tried to eat me. I thought very hard about needing a barrier, and a barrier appeared.

Then it disappeared.

Now my arm was repairing itself while making sure I appreciated every second.

Perfect.

Completely normal sequence of events.

I laughed once.

It came out weaker than I wanted.

I opened my eyes and stared into the trees.

For the first time since waking, I did not feel like performing.

Not for myself.

Not even for you.

I was scared.

There.

Enjoy the exclusive.

It lasted maybe three seconds.

Then I stood, because fear is useful right up until it becomes furniture.

My arm still hurt. The cuts had not vanished, but they looked hours or days older than they should have. My body felt heavy too, like the strange healing had taken something out of me.

I followed the old road more carefully after that.

No heroic marching.

No dramatic declarations.

I listened before every blind turn and gave thick brush considerably more respect than I had fifteen minutes earlier.

The route slowly improved. Broken stones became a clearer path. The drainage ditch reappeared in sections. I found wheel marks pressed into softer ground, recent enough that rain had not erased the edges.

Then I saw the first maintained post.

Fresh-cut timber.

A metal band around its base.

Several symbols had been carved into a board above it.

I could not read a single one.

That was almost comforting.

At least one thing today was behaving normally.

Below the writing was a simple carved symbol: a cluster of roof shapes.

An arrow pointed along the better road.

Settlement.

People.

Food, maybe.

Answers, ideally.

New and exciting ways to get arrested, statistically possible.

I stood at the junction for a moment.

Behind me waited the old road, the forest, and at least one animal that had already reviewed me poorly.

Ahead was a road used recently by carts, boots, and hoofed animals.

Whatever civilization looked like here, it existed.

I checked my arm again.

The wound was still there.

It was also undeniably smaller.

I flexed my hand and felt pain pull along the healing cuts. Then I held out the other hand and imagined the slab again.

Nothing.

Not even a dramatic spark.

“Fine.”

Probably for the best.

If I had accidentally become all-powerful in the first hour, you and I would be trapped in a very short story.

I adjusted my clothes, wiped away as much dirt as possible, and straightened my back.

Nobody was watching yet.

That was not the point.

The forest had gotten the first impression.

Civilization would get a better one.

I stepped onto the maintained road and followed the arrow.

Whatever waited ahead, it had people.

And people, unlike monsters, could usually be reasoned with.

Usually.
