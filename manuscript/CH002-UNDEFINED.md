# Chapter 2 — Undefined

Civilization announced itself with smoke, wagon wheels, and a wall tall enough to make me reconsider how dangerous the forest behind me actually was.

The road curved down through thinning trees, and Greywake—though I did not know the name yet—rose out of the river fog ahead. Timber palisades followed the edge of the settlement, reinforced with darker beams and squat watch platforms. Carts waited in two lines outside the gate while people in travel-stained coats argued, unloaded crates, checked harnesses, and generally behaved like being alive was a scheduled activity.

I stopped at the edge of the road and took a moment to compose myself.

My sleeve was torn. There was dried blood on my forearm. Mud had formed what I can only describe as a political alliance with the rest of my clothes.

Still, first impressions matter.

I straightened my back, rolled one shoulder, and walked toward the gate like I had arrived exactly where I intended.

You know the technique. If you look lost, people ask questions. If you look mysterious, people also ask questions, but at least the lighting feels better.

Two guards noticed me before I reached the first wagon.

One was broad enough to make the spear in his hand look decorative. The other was younger, narrower, and already frowning at the blood on my sleeve. They exchanged a few words.

I understood none of them.

The broad guard raised one hand. “Vara sen tal. Keth orren?”

I gave him my most reasonable expression.

“I'm going to assume that means welcome.”

His frown deepened.

The younger guard said something faster. I caught exactly zero useful syllables.

There went the dramatic entrance.

I pointed at myself. “Red Jackal.”

Then I pointed through the gate.

The guards looked at each other.

I pointed through the gate again, slower this time, because apparently that is how language works.

The younger guard sighed, turned toward a small booth built into the inner side of the gate, and called something over his shoulder. A tired-looking attendant emerged carrying a round brass token on a cord.

That got my attention.

The token was about the size of a large coin, etched with thin circular lines. The attendant held it between us, pressed a thumb to the center, and a faint vibration passed through the air.

Not light. Not sound exactly.

More like the pressure change before someone speaks close to your ear.

“Try again,” the attendant said.

I blinked.

The words were not English. I could hear that much. The sounds were different, the rhythm was different, but the meaning arrived anyway.

I looked at the token.

Then at him.

“That is either very useful or extremely invasive.”

He glanced at the token as if I had complimented a spoon. “Wayfarer tongue token. It handles speech. Not thoughts.”

“Good distinction.”

“It also does not translate writing.”

I glanced at the boards fixed beside the gate, all covered in the same symbols I had failed to read on the road.

“Less good distinction.”

The attendant ignored that. Professionals are dangerous like that.

“What is your name?”

“Red Jackal.”

He waited.

I waited back.

“Your name,” he repeated.

“That was the answer.”

The younger guard made a small sound that the token translated mostly as disbelief.

The attendant rubbed his forehead. “Fine. Claimed name: Red Jackal. Origin?”

That was a worse question.

I could have said Earth.

I could have said another world.

I could have told them my last memory involved a road, rain, headlights, and an argument with physics that I had apparently lost.

Instead, I looked toward the forest behind me.

“Complicated.”

The attendant stared at me.

“That is not an origin.”

“I noticed.”

“Travel party?”

“None.”

“Papers?”

“No.”

“Guild seal?”

“No.”

“Local sponsor?”

“Not yet.”

Each answer seemed to remove another small piece of his remaining optimism.

The broad guard looked at my torn sleeve. “Weapon?”

“Currently? No.”

That answer was technically true, which is my favorite kind of true.

The attendant exhaled through his nose. “You came out of the north road alone, carrying no papers, with no guild mark and no recognized sponsor.”

“Yes.”

“And you're injured.”

I looked at my forearm. The cuts were still visible, but compared with what they had been less than an hour ago, they looked insultingly minor. Sore, tight, and tender, yes. Freshly mauled by a forest predator? Not anymore.

“It's improving.”

The guard looked unconvinced.

I smiled. “Excellent body maintenance.”

Nobody laughed.

Fine. Tough crowd.

The attendant pointed toward the gate. “This is Greywake. Entry is controlled. If you have no papers, we check registry.”

Greywake.

There it was. A name at last.

I glanced past him at the fog-softened roofs inside the palisade. “Greywake. Nice. Slightly threatening. Very frontier.”

“Do you want to enter or review the naming?”

“Enter.”

“Then stand on the seal.”

Set into the stone beside the gate was a circular plate I had assumed was decoration. It was about a meter across, made of dark metal marked with thin geometric grooves. Travelers ahead of me had stepped over it without ceremony.

One merchant crossed. The grooves lit faintly. The attendant looked at a smaller metal indicator beside the post, nodded, and waved him through.

Routine.

That was important.

Nobody gasped. Nobody prayed. Nobody announced the arrival of destiny.

Whatever this thing was, it was as normal to them as showing an ID card.

I stepped onto it.

The grooves brightened under my boots.

For half a second, nothing happened.

Then the light stopped.

The attendant frowned.

The younger guard leaned closer to the side indicator.

The attendant tapped it once. “Stay there.”

“I was planning something more dramatic, but sure.”

He reset the seal and tried again.

The lines lit.

Stopped.

A small mark flashed on the side indicator.

The attendant's expression changed.

“What?”

He did not answer immediately.

That helped my aura tremendously.

The younger guard shifted his grip on his spear.

Now we were getting somewhere.

I let the silence sit for one extra second and lowered my voice. “Something unusual?”

The attendant gave me a look that ruined the entire effect.

“It says System Recognition: Failed.”

I kept my face still.

System.

There it was.

Finally.

I had been waiting for this since I woke up in the forest. Every respectable other-world disaster was supposed to come with some kind of System eventually.

Apparently mine had arrived late and immediately rejected my application.

The attendant checked the indicator again. “World Registry: No Record.”

The broad guard's posture changed slightly.

I folded my arms, then regretted it when the healing cuts pulled.

“So,” I said, “the world itself has no record of me.”

The attendant looked at me flatly. “It means I cannot finish the entry form.”

There are few things more damaging to a mysterious revelation than a civil servant with a missing field.

The younger guard was still watching me differently now. Not terrified. Just more carefully.

Partial success.

I'll take it.

“What does it normally say?” I asked.

“Recognized. Then it returns the entry categories we need.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“We verify separately.”

“Of course you do.”

Because why have one bureaucracy when you can have a backup bureaucracy?

The attendant motioned me off the seal. “Could be the ward. Could be interference. Could be damaged relation data. Could be something from a remote registration tradition. We don't decide from one failed gate check.”

Reasonable people.

Annoying.

I had been prepared for superstition. I was less prepared for troubleshooting.

They moved me into a narrow intake room built beside the gate. A single desk divided the space. Hooks held blank wooden tags, stamped cords, and several objects whose purposes I could only guess.

More writing covered the wall.

Still unreadable.

The Wayfarer token hung from a peg between us, its faint hum continuing while the attendant fetched someone from the next room.

A woman in a grey-brown clerk's coat returned with him, carrying a rectangular slate framed in metal. Unlike the gate seal, this thing looked deliberately portable. Lines were engraved around its edges, and a narrow inset panel occupied the center.

She set it on the desk.

“Certified identity slate,” she said. “Separate query from the gate ward.”

“Meaning if this also hates me, we can stop blaming the floor?”

She paused. “That is one way to phrase it.”

I liked her already.

The clerk gestured toward the slate. “Hand above the center. Do not touch the frame.”

I obeyed.

The panel clouded.

Then symbols appeared.

I could not read them.

This was becoming a recurring theme.

The clerk could.

Her eyes moved once across the slate.

Then back to the first line.

Then down again, more slowly.

“What?” I asked.

She did not answer.

A guard behind me shifted his weight.

Excellent atmosphere.

I turned slightly toward you, metaphorically speaking. If a mysterious artifact fails to identify you and everyone in the room goes quiet, there are two available strategies.

Panic.

Or commit to the entrance.

I chose the one with better posture.

“Well,” I said, “I assume you've discovered I'm extremely important.”

The clerk looked up.

“I have discovered that your form is unusable.”

Brutal.

She looked back down. “Claimed name?”

“Red Jackal.”

“I can write that manually.”

“See? Progress.”

“The System Name field is undefined.”

My smile stayed in place.

Something underneath it did not.

“Undefined how?”

She glanced toward the Wayfarer token, then back to me. “Undefined as in the slate cannot return a valid defined value.”

“Hidden?”

“No.”

“Blocked?”

“No.”

“Restricted?”

“No.”

That answer landed harder than I expected.

The clerk continued, now reading the results with the careful voice of someone confirming a problem she did not want to confirm.

“Race: undefined. Class: undefined. Level: undefined.”

The younger guard stopped pretending not to listen.

“HP and MP are also undefined,” she said. “Progression unavailable.”

I stared at the slate even though the symbols meant nothing to me.

There is a special kind of discomfort in watching someone read information about you that you cannot read yourself.

It got worse.

“System Recognition failed,” she said. “Classification... anomaly.”

That one had style.

I almost appreciated it.

“World Registry: no record.”

The clerk hesitated.

“Soul Registry: no record.”

The room became very quiet.

For once, I did not help.

A joke arrived. Several, actually.

I could have used any of them.

None felt quite right.

The first device had failed. Fine. Machines fail. Magic circles fail. People hit things and try again.

This was something else.

A separate tool had asked a deeper question and received the same answer in more detail.

Not hidden.

Not sealed.

Not classified above their access.

Undefined.

No record.

I was standing in the room. I was breathing. My arm hurt. I had walked here on my own two feet.

And according to whatever structure these people trusted to define such things, I apparently lacked the courtesy to exist correctly.

The clerk looked at me. “Do you know why this is happening?”

“No.”

That answer needed no performance.

She studied me for another second, then wrote something by hand on a paper form.

I couldn't read it, but she said it aloud as she worked.

“Claimed name or alias: Red Jackal.”

“Alias?”

“Unless the System recognizes it as your Name, yes.”

I leaned back slightly. “So I can tell you who I am, but your universe refuses to sign the paperwork.”

“The System is not the universe.”

Good to know.

“Then your System refuses to sign the paperwork.”

“That is closer.”

I filed away the correction.

The door opened behind us.

The woman who entered was not dressed like the gate guards, though the way both of them straightened told me she did not need matching armor to outrank them.

She was in her late thirties, maybe. Practical coat, reinforced boots, short weapon at her side. Nothing ornamental. Her eyes went first to the clerk, then the slate, then me.

Efficient order.

“Captain Alder,” the clerk said.

So. Authority.

Captain Brynn Alder took the situation in without rushing. “Summary.”

The clerk gave it to her in less than half a minute.

No papers. Unknown origin. Cooperative. Gate Registry Seal failed. Certified Identity Slate failed separately. Claimed alias Red Jackal. No registry relation. Undefined standard fields.

Brynn looked at me. “You understand me?”

I glanced at the brass token. “With assistance.”

“Good. Where did you enter the north road?”

“I found an older road in the forest and followed it until it became a better road.”

“Before that?”

“Forest.”

Her expression did not change.

“Yes, I heard that part.”

I considered how much truth would improve my position.

Not much.

“I woke up there.”

“After what?”

“A very bad night.”

Not technically false.

Brynn looked at the blood on my sleeve. “Were you attacked?”

“Yes.”

“By what?”

“I'd love to know.”

“You need a healer?”

My arm gave a small pulse of soreness as if voting yes.

I ignored it.

“I can move it. Bleeding stopped.”

Brynn's eyes dropped briefly to the visible marks, then returned to my face. She did not ask why they looked better than the dried blood suggested they should.

Good.

One mystery at a time.

A second woman stood just outside the intake desk now. I had noticed her earlier only as another person with papers, but she was watching the slate rather than me.

Dark practical clothing. Neat posture. No weapon visible. The kind of expression people wear when the facts are misbehaving.

Brynn noticed her too. “You have a question, Orra?”

The woman nodded once. “One.”

Her attention shifted to the clerk. “The gate seal and that slate are separate authorized interfaces?”

“Yes.”

“Separate local query paths?”

“Yes.”

“And neither produced a valid registry relation?”

The clerk's mouth tightened. “Correct.”

The woman looked at me then.

Not frightened.

Interested.

Somehow, that was worse.

“Maelis Orra,” she said. “Ledger of Measures.”

“Red Jackal. Apparently.”

One corner of her mouth almost moved.

Almost.

Brynn looked between us. “This is still my gate.”

Maelis dipped her head. “I know.”

“Good.”

I liked Brynn too.

She rested one hand on the edge of the desk. “Here's the problem, Red Jackal. The System does not identify you. That means the gate cannot verify your history, entry category, guild standing, legal notices, or travel relation.”

“I don't have any of those.”

“That does not improve the problem.”

Fair.

“You've cooperated,” she continued. “You haven't threatened anyone. You came in on foot, you're unarmed, and I don't have grounds to put you in a cell because a registry tool failed.”

The younger guard visibly relaxed.

A little.

Brynn continued before I could enjoy that victory.

“I also don't have grounds to treat you like a verified traveler.”

There it was.

Freedom from classification. Very dramatic.

Freedom from the protections attached to classification.

Less dramatic.

“So what's the compromise?”

Brynn looked at the clerk. “Unverified entrant.”

The clerk nodded and reached for one of the blank wooden tags hanging beside the desk.

It was flat, narrow, and stamped with a simple gate emblem. She marked it manually, threaded a dark cord through the top, and slid it toward me.

“Temporary,” Brynn said. “Keep it with you.”

I picked it up.

It did not glow.

No hidden title appeared.

No voice announced a new achievement.

Just wood, cord, stamp, and apparently my entire legal existence.

“This is deeply humbling.”

“It should be.”

Brynn listed the restrictions without ceremony.

If militia or gate staff challenged me, I had to show the token. I could enter the settlement, buy ordinary goods, rent a room if someone was willing to take my money—which introduced the exciting new problem that I had no money—and move through unrestricted public areas.

I could not accept standard registered guild contracts.

I could not use identity-bound gate or travel services.

I would not receive normal registered-traveler guarantees or insurance.

And at the next intake cycle, I had to return for a controlled follow-up identity check.

“That last one with her?” I asked, glancing at Maelis.

Maelis answered before Brynn did. “I'd like to observe it.”

“Observe?”

“And verify.”

“That sounds less relaxing.”

“It isn't intended to be relaxing.”

Brynn gave Maelis a brief look, then turned back to me. “You report back. You cooperate. If you cause trouble, the token makes it easier for my people to know which unverified stranger they're looking for.”

I held up the wooden tag.

“So this is not a badge of honor.”

“No.”

“Special access?”

“No.”

“Mysterious exemption from local law?”

“No.”

I sighed. “You people are very committed to ruining the branding.”

The clerk finally laughed.

Quietly.

Victory.

Brynn did not, but I saw the edge of the tension leave her shoulders.

The clerk started organizing the failed forms into a separate stack. One copy went into a gate file. Another, she told Brynn, would pass through guild administration because my restrictions blocked ordinary contract processing.

I did not know who would read it.

At the time, I did not care.

I had bigger concerns.

Food.

Money.

A roof.

And the small issue of reality itself apparently returning an error when asked who I was.

The gate opened to the inner lane.

I stepped through.

Greywake was not grand.

That was the first thing I noticed.

It was alive.

Timber buildings leaned close along packed-earth streets. Lantern bridges crossed narrow drainage channels. Carts creaked through river mist. Somewhere nearby, somebody was hammering metal, and somewhere even closer somebody was cooking something that smelled unfairly good.

People moved with the practical speed of a place that expected work to be done before weather changed its mind.

Nobody stopped to welcome me.

Perfect.

For the first time since waking in that forest, I was surrounded by people.

I should have felt safer.

Instead, the wooden token in my hand suddenly felt heavier than it had any right to.

The forest had tried to eat me.

Greywake had done something stranger.

It had asked the world who I was.

The world had answered:

No record.
