# Chapter 5 — A Better Hammer

**Status:** CANON — GATE 9 PASS / DEADPAN VOICE LOCKED  
**Word Count:** 3,105

First light was a cruel phrase. It suggested something quiet and poetic. What it actually meant was standing in the Road Guild caravan yard before I had found a reliable source of breakfast. I arrived with the wooden entrant token under my coat, the bridge-marked assignment card in one pocket, and the remaining advance in another. The sky was still grey above Greywake, and the yard was already awake.

Workers were loading timber onto a wagon. Two guards checked spearheads near the gate. Someone was arguing about rope. Apparently roads did not maintain themselves just because people found them convenient. A broad-shouldered man with a weather-dark coat looked at my assignment card, then at me.

He was probably in his forties. Maybe older. Hard to tell with people who spent most of their lives outside.

“You Red Jackal?”

The Wayfarer Tongue Token hanging from the side of the repair wagon carried the meaning clearly.

“That is what they keep telling me.”

He handed the card back.

“Foreman.”

No name. Just foreman. Efficient. He pointed at a stack of iron clamps beside the wagon.

“Carry those.”

I looked at the clamps. Then at him. Then back at the clamps. So much for mysterious irregular frontier work. I picked them up.

They were heavier than they looked. The foreman had already moved on. No questions about my missing class. No concern about my undefined level. No interest in what the System thought of me.

I had two arms and had shown up on time. Apparently that was enough qualification for road maintenance. The repair crew left Greywake just after sunrise. There were six workers, two assigned road guards, the foreman, one wagon, and me. The Wayfarer Tongue Token hung from a hook beneath the wagon roof so the crew could speak to me when needed.

Without it, their Valic turned back into unfamiliar sound. I still could not read the painted route boards we passed. That remained my problem. The Main Caravan Road was wider than I had realized on the walk into Greywake. Outside the gate, the packed surface had been raised above the surrounding ground with layers of gravel and timber reinforcement. Drainage ditches ran along both sides. Stone markers stood at regular intervals. Every few hundred paces, I saw evidence of repair—fresh fill, replaced boards, cleared brush, reinforced culverts.

Keeping a road open apparently required a large number of people preventing nature from taking it back. One of the workers noticed me looking at a line of shallow channels cut beside the road.

“Flood drains.”

“I assumed decorative trenches would be an unusual frontier tradition.”

He stared at me. The token translated the silence perfectly. I stopped trying. We passed two caravans heading toward Greywake. Both slowed as they approached us, drivers exchanging short words with the foreman.

The road mattered. That was obvious. Every wagon carrying grain, tools, cloth, medicine, or people depended on this strip of maintained ground not turning into mud or monster territory. Greywake's walls were useful. The road was probably why the walls had anything inside them.

The damaged bridge approach appeared around midmorning. The bridge itself crossed a narrow river channel, low and broad rather than dramatic. No deep gorge. No raging waterfall. Nothing worth falling into for narrative purposes.

The problem was on the near side. Recent rain had softened the roadbed and pushed one of the approach supports out of alignment. A heavy timber brace beneath the outer edge had swollen with water and shifted far enough that the surface above it sagged slightly.

Not enough to collapse. Enough that sending loaded wagons across it would be stupid. The crew immediately started working. Nobody stood around waiting for me to solve anything. Two workers unloaded replacement boards and wedges. Another cleared packed mud from around the support. The foreman checked the angle with a long metal measuring bar etched with narrow geometric markings.

The markings gave off a faint blue glow. I looked closer.

Sigils.

I still could not read them. A worker set an iron clamp around the damaged brace. Small etched lines along the clamp lit as he tightened it. The timber stopped shifting. So this was what ordinary practical magic looked like here.

Prepared tool. Prepared markings. Specific job. No dramatic chanting. Honestly, it was disappointingly sensible.

The foreman caught me watching.

“Alignment clamp.”

“I gathered.”

“Don't touch the marks.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“You were staring at them.”

“I stare at many things I don't understand.”

He accepted that. The crew spent the next hour forcing the swollen brace back into position. I carried timber. Held one end of a support beam. Dragged a coil of rope.

Moved two crates because someone pointed at them. Frontier work continued to be less glamorous than advertised. The important part was that the crew knew exactly what they were doing. The foreman called positions. Two workers levered the brace.

Another checked the alignment bar. One guard remained near the road while the other watched the tree line. The whole thing moved with the practiced irritation of people who had fixed similar problems before. Eventually, the brace settled into the right position.

A worker slid a thick iron pin through the first half of the joint. It stopped. The swollen timber held it tight. The foreman checked the angle again.

“Needs driving.”

One of the workers went to the wagon and came back with a heavy two-handed hammer.

Finally.

A tool I understood. He raised it and brought it down on the end of the pin. Metal rang. The pin moved slightly. Second strike.

Another small movement. Third strike— The hammer handle split near the head. The metal head twisted sideways and hit the ground. Everyone stopped. The worker holding the broken handle looked at it.

The foreman closed his eyes for half a second. I understood that expression. It was the universal face of equipment failure.

“Spare?” one of the workers asked.

“Smaller.”

“How much smaller?”

The foreman looked at the wagon.

“Enough to make this take longer.”

Traffic had already begun stacking up on the far side of the work zone. Three wagons waited under guard. Another had stopped behind them. The foreman pointed at two workers.

“Reset the brace. Keep pressure on it.”

Then to another.

“Get the smaller maul.”

Nobody panicked. Nobody declared disaster. They had a fallback. It was just slower. I looked at the broken hammer head lying in the mud.

Then at the iron pin. Then at my hands. The thought came before I invited it. The slab. In the forest, I had needed something between myself and teeth.

Something had appeared. Later, when I tried to make it happen again, nothing. At the time I had assumed fear mattered. Maybe it did. But the first thought had also been specific.

I need something between us. Not shield. Not weapon. A function. I looked at the bridge pin again.

The crew needed a hammer. That was simple. Possibly too simple. I should probably have tested this somewhere private. Not beside six workers, two guards, a damaged bridge, and several waiting caravans. If you're thinking that made this a poor testing environment, I had reached the same conclusion.

On the other hand, I had not found a private place with a broken heavy hammer and an immediate reason to care. Research conditions were imperfect. I stepped away from the crew. The foreman noticed.

“What are you doing?”

“Trying something.”

“That usually means I should stop you.”

“Probably.”

He frowned. I held out one hand. I remembered the feeling from the forest. Not mana. At least I did not think it was mana.

There had been no spell. No words. Just need. I focused. A hammer.

I need a hammer. Nothing happened. I kept the thought steady. Hammer. Something solid.

Something I could use. For a second, I felt the strange pressure again. Reality seemed to catch on something. Then an object dropped into my hand. I almost lost it.

Not because it was heavy. Because it was ridiculous. It was a hammer. Technically. The handle was too short.

The head was wide but thin, more like someone had described a hammer to a child and received an enthusiastic drawing in return. I stared at it. The foreman stared at it. One of the guards took a step closer.

“Well,” I said.

Nobody helped. I had asked for a hammer. I had received a hammer. That was apparently the full extent of the agreement. I walked over to the brace-pin.

The foreman moved in front of me.

“Wait.”

He looked at the object. Then at my hand. Then at the ground where it definitely had not been a moment earlier.

“What casting was that?”

“I was hoping you knew.”

The nearby guard frowned.

“No sigil.”

“No.”

“No focus tool.”

“No.”

“What words did you use?”

“None.”

His expression changed. Not fear. Confusion.

“That isn't how Guild shaping works.”

Useful.

Not comforting.

The foreman pointed at the hammer.

“Can it drive the pin?”

Only one way to find out. I raised it. The balance was terrible. The head pulled sideways. I struck the iron pin anyway.

The result was a small metallic tap. The pin did not move. The hammer head bent. Not a lot. Enough.

I looked at it. The worker beside me looked at it too.

“That is a bad hammer,” he said.

“Thank you.”

The surface blurred. The head thinned. A moment later, the entire thing softened into nothing. That part was familiar. The crew remained silent.

I was becoming very good at creating those. The foreman looked at me.

“Can you do that again?”

There was the question.

“I don't know.”

Honest answers were becoming inconvenient. The worker with the smaller maul returned. The foreman took it.

“Then we use this.”

He turned away. That should have been the end of the experiment. It was not. Because the first attempt had worked. Badly.

But it had worked. I had wanted a hammer. I got one. Not the hammer. A hammer.

The distinction bothered me. The foreman handed the smaller maul to a worker. The worker took position. The first strike barely moved the pin. Second strike.

Almost nothing. The brace creaked under tension.

“Hold it,” the foreman said.

Two workers leaned harder on the support. One of the guards suddenly raised a hand.

“Movement.”

Everything changed quickly. The second guard turned toward the trees. The foreman pointed.

“Workers in.”

Nobody argued. The crew near the outer edge shifted closer to the wagon. A low shape moved through the brush beyond the drainage ditch. Then another. Then a third.

They were smaller than the thing that had attacked me in the forest, lean-bodied and long-legged, with grey-brown hides that disappeared easily against wet bark. Ordinary predators.

Probably.

I had learned not to make strong claims about wildlife. One of the guards stepped forward with his spear. The other drew a short blade. The animals did not charge immediately. They circled.

Noise, food, exposed workers. Easy opportunity. The guard nearest me glanced back.

“Stay with the crew.”

Reasonable instruction. I looked at the brace-pin. Still halfway in. If the crew released pressure now, the swollen timber would shift again. Not catastrophic.

Just more work. The foreman saw the same thing.

“Keep the brace set.”

Two workers held position. The smaller maul came down again. The pin moved less than an inch. One of the predators darted toward the supply wagon. The guard intercepted it.

Spear point flashed. The animal twisted away. The second predator moved from the opposite side. Both guards shifted. They could handle them.

Probably.

The problem was time. The brace needed one heavy strike. Not a hammer. Not just a hammer. The exact thing.

I looked at the iron pin. At the swollen timber. At the workers holding the brace. Then I stopped thinking about the word. I pictured the result instead.

A hammer I could actually swing. Heavy enough to drive that pin through the swollen brace.

One clean strike.

That was all. The pressure returned. Stronger this time. Something formed in my hands. I immediately regretted adding any sense of style to the image.

The handle was long. Too long. The head was enormous. Not impossibly enormous. Just large enough that a reasonable person would have chosen something smaller.

Dark material formed along the surface in uneven planes, more solid than the first hammer and much heavier. The weight dragged my arms down. One of the workers stared.

“That is not better.”

“It might be.”

The guard beside the road looked over. Again, no sigil. No spell. No preparation. Just me holding something that had not existed a second earlier.

I did not have time to think about what that meant. The hammer was already starting to feel unstable. I stepped toward the pin. The foreman looked at the hammer, then the brace, then me.

“Can you control it?”

“Enough.”

Probably.

He pointed to the workers.

“Hold.”

They braced. I raised the hammer. Bad idea. My shoulders immediately objected. The thing was much heavier than I had intended.

Clear function, apparently. Reasonable proportions were optional. I adjusted my grip. The head trembled slightly. Focus.

One strike. Drive the pin. That was the purpose. I swung. The hammer came down.

The impact cracked through the worksite. The iron pin drove through the swollen timber and seated hard against the outer plate. For half a second, nobody moved. Then the foreman shouted.

“Clamp it.”

The crew reacted immediately. Two workers secured the brace. Another locked the outer plate. The enchanted clamp flared blue. The structure settled.

My arms felt like they had been replaced with rope. The hammer was still there. Barely. One of the predators came too close around the wagon. The nearest guard was occupied with another.

I turned. The animal lowered itself. I did not have enough control left for anything clever. Fortunately, I was holding a hammer the size of a bad decision. I shifted the shaft sideways and let the head drop between the animal and the workers.

The creature checked its charge.

Good.

I shoved the hammer forward. The head clipped the ground, kicked up mud, and forced the animal back. Not elegant. Effective enough. The guard moved in and drove it away with the spear.

The rest of the small pack followed once they realized breakfast had developed organization. Nobody chased them. The guards held position until the brush went still. I looked down at the hammer. Cracks were moving across the dark surface.

That seemed bad. I let go. The handle softened before it hit the ground. The head sagged. Then the whole thing collapsed into nothing.

My knees almost did the same. I caught the edge of the wagon. The foreman looked at me.

“You all right?”

“Define all right.”

“Standing.”

“Yes.”

“Then sit down.”

That was probably wise. I sat on a timber stack while the crew finished securing the bridge approach. My hands shook slightly. My shoulders hurt. There was a dull pressure behind my eyes that made focusing unpleasant.

So. Intentional manifestation was possible. That was new. The first result had been useless because I had asked for a category. Hammer.

The second had worked because I had asked for a purpose. Drive the pin.

One clean strike.

The shape had changed with the thought. Not perfectly. Definitely not politely. But enough. Clear idea.

Better result. I stored that away.

Not a rule yet.

Maybe a pattern.

The guard who had asked about the casting walked over. He looked at the empty patch of mud where the hammer had disappeared.

“What was that?”

“I was hoping you had a second theory.”

“I have none.”

“Good. That makes two of us.”

He glanced toward the alignment clamp.

“That wasn't sigilcraft.”

“I know.”

“You didn't shape through a tool.”

“No.”

“No spoken pattern.”

“No.”

He looked at me for another second.

“Strange magic.”

“That's currently the leading explanation.”

He walked away. Not exactly a scholarly breakthrough. Still useful. The crew finished the repair before noon. They replaced the damaged surface boards, checked the brace twice, reset the drainage edge, and tested the approach with the unloaded repair wagon before allowing normal traffic through.

Nobody asked me to hit anything else. Probably sensible. The foreman walked the repaired section once, checked the alignment bar, then waved the first waiting caravan forward. The wagon crossed slowly. The bridge held.

The workers relaxed. Just slightly. The important part was done. My impossible hammer had driven one pin. The crew had repaired the bridge.

That distinction mattered. On the return trip, I carried less. This may have been sympathy. I chose to interpret it as professional recognition. The Wayfarer Token swung beneath the wagon roof while the crew talked around me.

Nobody called me chosen. Nobody asked whether I had a legendary class. One worker asked whether I could make a shovel next time. I told him no. Mostly because I did not know.

The foreman submitted the completion record at the Road Guild yard that afternoon. A clerk checked the stamped assignment card, counted returned supplies, listened to the foreman's report, and opened a cash drawer. I stood there trying not to look too interested.

The clerk placed the remaining payment in Marks on the counter. I counted them. Not because I had mastered local economics overnight. Because counting money was universal enough.

“Complete,” the clerk said.

That was it. No celebration. No ceremony. No System notification. I had helped repair a bridge, frightened off one predator with an oversized temporary hammer, and discovered that reality responded differently depending on how I framed what I wanted.

The Guild cared that the bridge was open. Honestly, I respected that. I put the Marks away. The foreman was already speaking to another worker about tomorrow's traffic. To him, my impossible hammer was apparently less important than whether six wagons could cross before dark.

Reasonable priorities. I stepped out into the yard. My shoulders still ached. My head felt heavy. Trying the strange ability again sounded like an excellent way to learn what unconsciousness felt like, so I decided against it.

For now. I had money. I had completed the job. And I had learned one thing. Vague wishing got me something technically correct.

A clear purpose got me something useful. That was not an explanation. It was enough to work with. Frontier employment was beginning to look more interesting. Mostly because the tools occasionally came from nowhere.
