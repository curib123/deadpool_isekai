# Chapter 16 — Backflow

The calls did not stop.

That was the first thing.

The lure lay dead in the stone recess behind us. Its rear plate had gone dark. The vent no longer hummed. The Guild field crew had already pulled back toward Greywake to report what I had done.

Tavian stayed where he was. He listened. A charged Wayfarer Tongue Token hung from his belt, but nobody was talking.

The forest was. The first call came from the north-east. Another answered from lower ground.

Then something moved through the brush west of the upper approach.

Not one animal.

Several.

Branches shook in a line that crossed the road instead of following it. Kellan turned toward the farms.

“What is that?”

Tavian raised one hand. We waited. A group of long-legged browsing animals broke from the trees and crossed the upper road at a run. I had seen tracks like theirs before. Heavy bodies. Narrow heads. Built to eat plants and avoid trouble.

They were doing badly at the second part. They came from the screened side of the dead lure. Straight across the road.

One stumbled against the drainage stones, recovered, and kept running. Behind them came a sound I knew. Ridge-hounds.

Not the two from before. More. Tavian moved immediately.

“Downhill. Now.”

Kellan looked at him.

“Back to the farms?”

“No. Off this line.”

I looked toward the trees.

“How many?”

“Doesn't matter.”

That answer did. The ridge-hounds were not charging us. They were following the movement.

One crossed the road where the prey herd had crossed. Another came out farther south. A third call sounded beyond both.

Tavian grabbed my arm and pulled me away from the center of the approach.

“Do not engage unless they turn on people.”

“They are moving toward people.”

“Then we move the people.”

I looked back toward the lure. For one second, I wanted there to be something obvious to hit. A center.

A source. One thing I could break harder than I had broken the last one. There wasn't.

Another line of movement cut through the lower brush. Smaller animals this time. Fast. Scattered. Running across the road at angles that made no sense beside the larger herd.

Tavian watched them. His face changed.

Not fear.

Recognition.

“Backflow.”

Kellan looked at him.

“You're sure?”

“Enough.”

The word settled harder than certainty would have. Tavian pointed downhill.

“Those routes were screened before. They're not settling into an old path. They're spilling into available ones.”

The ridge-hounds crossed again. Not one route.

Several.

I felt the wound along my side tighten as I started moving. Pain came with it. The bite in my leg had stopped bleeding, but every hard step pulled at the half-healed tissue.

My body was correcting damage. It was not replacing energy. We ran.

The first farm approach had already started emptying by the time we reached it. People were carrying what they could. Not everything.

Nobody ever carried everything. A woman dragged two sacks toward a cart while another person pulled a child away from a livestock pen. Someone had cut the pen gate open instead of trying to lead the animals one by one.

Good decision. The animals scattered anyway. Kellan shouted directions.

“Lower connector. Leave the north track.”

People listened to him. That mattered more than anything I could say. Tavian climbed onto the low stone edge beside the road and looked across the fields.

“Do not use the tree-line path.”

Kellan turned.

“Why?”

“Movement behind it.”

He pointed. At first I saw nothing. Then the brush folded.

Small shapes came through. Not ridge-hounds. Lean scavengers or minor predators, too far away and moving too fast for me to identify.

They cut across a side track that had been empty the last time I was here. Another road gone. We reached the next junction just as three militia riders came in from Greywake.

The lead rider pulled up hard.

“Captain's coming.”

Tavian stepped into the road.

“Reports?”

“North farm approach. East timber connector. One movement line near the lower Greywake road.”

Kellan went still.

“The lower road?”

“Yes.”

“That was clear this morning.”

“Not now.”

Tavian looked toward Greywake. Then back toward the outer farms. The runner continued.

“Patrol markers are changing faster than we can send them.”

A second rider pointed behind them.

“Brynn is bringing two squads.”

Tavian nodded.

“Tell her this is backflow. Multiple corridors. Predator and prey movement overlapping.”

The rider did not argue. He turned his horse and left. That was when the scale became real.

Not the calls. Not the tracks. The fact that reports were arriving from roads we could not see.

I looked at the upper farms. Then the south connector. Then the road toward Greywake.

Too many directions.

I could run toward one. Maybe two. That was all.

Brynn arrived with militia on foot and horseback. She dismounted before the horse had fully settled.

“What moved?”

Tavian answered immediately.

“Several prey lines. Ridge-hounds behind at least one. Smaller predators using the side routes. Pressure is redistributing.”

“Cause?”

His eyes flicked toward me.

“Disabled lure is part of it.”

Brynn looked at me once. Nothing else. No accusation.

No hearing. Not now. She turned to her people.

“Split the squads. One to the lower Greywake approach. One with me.”

Then to the runner.

“Every farm cluster gets warning. Nobody waits for confirmation if they already have track movement.”

Kellan stepped forward.

“The seasonal-cut bridge.”

Brynn looked at him.

“What about it?”

“Fastest crossing for the west farms and timber sheds if the upper tracks are compromised.”

“Capacity?”

“People. Light loads. No heavy carts.”

“How many can move through?”

“Enough if they don't crowd it.”

Tavian looked toward the forest.

“They're going to crowd it.”

Kellan did not answer. Brynn did.

“Then we control the crossing.”

We moved. The road toward the bridge filled before we reached it. People from two directions joined the same connector.

Some carried packs. Some carried nothing. One man tried to lead a frightened mule while goats crowded behind it. A militia woman cut the mule's harness free when it twisted across the road.

The cart stayed. The people moved. A low thunder came from the trees.

Not one animal.

Many feet.

Tavian turned.

“Clear the road.”

Nobody needed more. The civilians moved toward the ditch and low field wall. I stayed in the road.

Tavian grabbed my shoulder.

“Not a fight.”

“I know.”

“Then move.”

A herd broke through the brush. Larger than the first group. They were not charging us.

They were running from something behind them. That made very little difference to a person standing in front of them. Two militia members dragged an older man over the field wall.

A cart wheel caught in a rut. Three people were still trying to move it. They were not going to make it.

I ran toward them. My side tore open again. Heat spread under my shirt.

The cart shifted half a foot. Not enough. The herd came closer.

Tavian shouted.

“Leave it.”

One person did. Two did not. I planted myself between the cart and the road.

I needed something simple. Not clever. Not large.

A wall would be too much. A wedge. A low barrier.

Something to make the front animals choose half a step sideways. That was all. I held the idea.

Low. Broad. Temporary.

A pale, uneven barrier formed across part of the road. Too short. I pushed the image wider.

It stretched another few feet and trembled. The edge wavered. My concentration slipped when pain pulled across my ribs.

I forced it back. The first animal reached the barrier. It checked.

The next hit its shoulder. The line bent. Not stopped.

Bent. The herd split around the low obstruction. Half went into the open field.

The other half crowded toward the ditch. Militia pulled the last two civilians clear. One animal clipped me as it passed.

The impact spun me into the road. My shoulder hit hard. The barrier flickered.

“Move!”

Tavian's voice. I rolled toward the ditch. The manifestation collapsed behind me.

Seconds.

That was all it had bought.

Seconds.

Enough for those people.

Not enough for the next road.

I tried to stand. My left arm shook. My side had started closing again under the blood.

My shoulder had not decided what it was doing yet. Kellan caught my good arm.

“Can you walk?”

“Yes.”

“Then walk.”

We did. Behind us, the herd kept moving. Ahead of us, another militia runner came from the direction of the bridge.

“Crossing is open. Too many people coming from the west.”

Kellan swore. We ran harder. Every time one route cleared, another report pointed to pressure somewhere people were already trying to survive. Roads connected farms. Animals followed open ground. Predators followed prey. People fled toward bridges. The reasons were ordinary, but the result kept tightening around occupied roads and crossings until it stopped feeling like escape and started feeling like being squeezed.

The bridge appeared beyond the timber sheds. I recognized it immediately.

Four supports.

Plank deck.

Far approach slightly lower than the rest.

Repairs on repairs.

The bridge Kellan had rebuilt because nobody else had sent enough material. There were more people on it now than I had ever seen. Kellan pushed ahead of us.

“Stop the carts.”

A driver shouted back.

“We need them across.”

“No.”

Kellan reached the near approach.

“Unload. People first.”

The driver looked at the animals and the road behind him.

“There isn't time.”

“Then leave the cart.”

A militia guard repeated it louder.

“Leave the cart.”

That worked. People started pulling sacks, children, tools, and water containers free. Some things stayed behind.

Kellan moved onto the bridge.

“Six at a time.”

Brynn arrived behind us with the second militia group. She saw the crossing once.

“Kellan?”

“Six. No more.”

She pointed to two militia members.

“Control the near side.”

Then two more.

“Far side. Keep them moving.”

Tavian stayed off the bridge. He watched the road. I knew what that meant.

The pressure was coming. Kellan kept calling the crossing.

“Next six.”

People moved.

“Wait.”

They stopped.

“Next.”

A family crossed. Then two workers. Then three children and an older woman.

The bridge flexed. Kellan felt it through his feet before I saw it.

“Slow.”

The next group slowed. A cart animal screamed behind us. Everyone turned.

A cluster of prey animals had broken onto the road from the timber side. Behind them, ridge-hounds. Militia lowered spears.

Tavian shouted.

“Do not close the road in front of the herd. Give them the field.”

Brynn pointed.

“Open the west fence.”

Two militia ran. One cut rope. Another kicked loose a simple rail.

The first prey animal turned toward the opening. The rest followed. Most of them.

One hit the abandoned cart. The cart lurched sideways. Its wheel climbed the edge of the bridge approach.

Kellan saw it.

“Get that off.”

Two people grabbed the cart frame. Another animal struck from behind. The cart moved another foot.

Onto the approach. Kellan jumped down.

“Leave it.”

The driver reached for the harness. Kellan shoved him away.

“Leave it.”

The cart animal pulled. The wheel jammed against the approach stone. The bridge shifted.

I heard the wood before I saw anything. A deep crack. Kellan looked under the deck.

“Everybody off.”

Brynn repeated it.

“Clear the bridge.”

People ran. That made the loading worse. Kellan stepped onto the deck.

“Not together. Space.”

Nobody listened at first. Panic had its own rules. He grabbed one man by the shoulder and physically stopped him.

“You. Wait.”

Then pointed.

“You three. Go.”

The bridge moved again. One of the four supports tilted. Not far.

Enough. Tavian looked at the road behind us.

“More movement.”

Brynn did not turn.

“How long?”

“Not long.”

The bridge had three people still on it. A child. A woman.

One militia guard. Kellan moved toward them. I followed.

He looked back.

“Stay off.”

“No.”

“You're heavy.”

That stopped me. He was right. I stayed at the approach.

The woman crossed. The child froze halfway. Kellan reached him.

The bridge gave another hard shift. The militia guard grabbed the child from the other side. Kellan pushed him toward the far bank.

The child cleared. Then the abandoned cart behind us broke free. Not toward the bridge.

Sideways. One wheel rolled off the packed edge. The frame twisted.

A loose timber beam strapped along the cart side swung outward. Someone shouted. Kellan turned.

The beam hit the near bridge rail. The rail broke. The impact went through the deck.

One repaired support snapped. The bridge dropped on one side. Kellan fell.

I moved before I understood where. The near planks tore loose. He hit the lower edge of the seasonal cut.

A section of deck came down after him. Then part of the cart frame. The sound was wrong.

Wood. Stone. A body underneath both.

I jumped down. Pain went through my leg when I landed. Kellan was conscious.

Barely. One leg was trapped below the knee beneath a broken deck beam and part of the cart frame. His other shoulder had struck the bank hard enough that he could not lift the arm properly.

Blood ran along the side of his face and down one sleeve. I grabbed the beam. It did not move.

Tavian dropped beside me.

“Together.”

A militia guard joined us. We lifted. My side tore again.

Something in my shoulder shifted. The beam rose an inch. Kellan made a sound through his teeth.

Not a scream.

Worse.

“Higher,” Tavian said.

We lifted. Another militia member pulled the cart frame away. Brynn climbed down and got both hands under Kellan's upper body.

“Now.”

We moved him. His trapped leg came free. It did not look right.

I looked away from it. Then back. He was bleeding.

Not the way I bled.

Not with skin already tightening under the blood.

Just bleeding.

I put pressure where Tavian told me. My hands were red in seconds. Kellan opened his eyes.

“Bridge?”

Nobody answered. Above us, another support failed. The remaining deck twisted and dropped into the cut.

The crossing was gone.

Not damaged.

Gone.

Brynn looked up.

“Can he be moved?”

Tavian checked Kellan quickly.

“Yes. Carefully.”

“Stretcher.”

Two militia climbed back to the road. I stayed beside him. My own side was already pulling shut again.

Slow.

Painful.

Still closing.

Kellan's leg did nothing. That difference sat in front of me where I could not ignore it. The stretcher came.

We lifted him onto it. He lost consciousness halfway through. Tavian checked his breathing.

“Still with us.”

Brynn looked at the road.

“What do we have left?”

Kellan could not answer now. A militia runner did.

“Lower farm track is still open on foot.”

“For how long?”

“No idea.”

Brynn pointed.

“Use it. No carts. Leave anything that slows the line.”

People started moving again. Around the broken bridge. Away from the road that had been carrying them home.

We climbed out of the cut. I looked back once. The bridge deck lay broken below the approach.

The repaired supports were split. The cart was half on its side.

The road ended there.

A horn sounded from farther east. Then another from the direction of Greywake. Brynn turned.

A rider came hard along the connector.

“Captain.”

“What?”

“Movement on the lower approach.”

“Animals?”

“Several lines. Not just predators.”

Tavian stepped closer.

“Direction?”

The rider pointed toward Greywake. Nobody spoke. Another horn answered from nearer the settlement.

The backflow was not staying out here. It was moving inward. Kellan lay on the stretcher behind me.

The bridge he had kept alive with reused wood and missing supplies was gone. I had not built Hesk's system. I had not spent years pushing animals into these roads.

But I had pulled one piece out while knowing I did not understand the rest.

My choice was in this chain.

Brynn started moving people toward the lower foot route. Tavian went with her. I followed.

Behind us, the bridge was lost.

Ahead of us, the migration was turning toward Greywake.
