# Chapter 26 — Before They Decide What I Am

**Status:** CLOSE-THIRD STYLE REVISION — GATE 9 PENDING
**Word Count:** 1,826

Greywake sounded different when nobody was screaming. Hammers had replaced alarm horns. Not everywhere.

One section of the outer approach still had militia markers blocking normal traffic, and the road toward Broken North remained closed behind a line of rope, warning boards, and workers who had developed the expression of people prepared to physically attack anyone who called a damaged road “probably fine.” He respected them. The market had reopened in pieces. Half the stalls were running. The other half had become storage points for repair timber, rope, tools, food, and whatever else Greywake had discovered it needed more urgently than decorative pottery.

Caravans still waited in the yard. Fewer were leaving. The ones that did moved under new route instructions that he could not read.

Some things survived every disaster. A clerk stood beside a route board reading the updated restrictions aloud while two people from the outer homesteads argued with a Guild representative about repair priority. The important part was that they were in the argument.

Not outside the room waiting to hear what had been decided for them. Brynn crossed the yard carrying three reports and looking like sleep had become a political disagreement.

“Broken North?”

“Still restricted.”

“Main road?”

“Limited traffic.”

“Council?”

“Still talking.”

“Terrible.”

“Yes.”

That was Greywake. Survival had apparently restored bureaucracy faster than infrastructure. She kept walking.

He followed the outer road far enough to see the damaged approaches from above. Fresh timber braced old stone. New marker posts stood beside broken ones. Workers had begun clearing debris from the seasonal-cut detour without pretending the route would reopen that day.

Greywake had survived. It looked like it. That was better than a clean ending.

Clean endings usually meant somebody had stopped counting. He went back to the treatment house because Sela had threatened to send someone after him if he did not. She inspected his side with the patience of a person who had none.

The wound had closed. The bruising had not. His ribs still complained when he twisted too far, and the leg he had abused through several chapters of bad judgment remained stiff.

Sela pressed near his shoulder. He moved away.

“Still hurts.”

“That was the purpose.”

“Medical science is cruel here.”

“Medical science is cruel everywhere.”

Fair. She checked his eyes, made him follow one finger, then watched his hands. The tremor was much smaller.

Still there.

“Better,” she said.

“That sounded almost positive.”

“Do not ruin it.”

Kellan was near the window with his leg still splinted and elevated. Someone had given him a stack of route sketches and apparently decided this counted as rest. He waved one sheet at him.

“They moved the northern repair priority.”

“Good?”

“Less stupid.”

High praise. He had not stood since the injury. He would not stand today either.

That mattered. Everyone else’s consequences did not disappear because his healed faster. Kellan put the paper down.

“You leaving?”

Sela looked at him before he answered. Apparently the treatment house had better intelligence than the militia.

“I was considering it.”

“You were considering it yesterday.”

“I consider things slowly.”

“No, you act quickly and explain it slowly.”

Sela made a sound that might have been agreement. He ignored both of them. Kellan looked toward the road outside.

“You stayed.”

That was not a question. He knew what he meant. After the backflow.

After his injury. After everything had become more difficult than breaking one bad thing.

“I did.”

He nodded once.

“That counts.”

Coming from him, it did. Then he picked up another route sketch. Conversation over.

Sela handed him a small wrapped packet of bandages.

“I am not taking those.”

“You are.”

“I regenerate.”

“You bleed first.”

He took the bandages. There were limits to personal agency. Some of them had Sela’s voice.

Maelis was in the records room with Jessa. That had become normal enough to be dangerous. The room held more copied documents than it had when he first arrived, except now the copies were not all controlled by the same office. Council seals, militia notices, Guild records, Ledger packets, and outer-community submissions occupied separate stacks across the long tables.

He still could not read any of them. His administrative development remained limited. Jessa was copying service references into a new record set.

“Temporary work?” he asked.

She nodded.

“Council records transition.”

“Permanent?”

“No.”

She said it without pretending not to care. That was progress too. Her siblings still needed money.

The Guild was being restructured around her. Doing the right thing had not created a better salary out of the air. Maelis closed one ledger.

“My formal report will take longer.”

“Because I am complicated?”

“Because Greywake is complicated.”

That was almost flattering. She pulled a smaller packet from the pile.

“This part is you.”

Less flattering.

“What did I do?”

“Exist.”

“Consistently?”

“So far.”

Jessa looked down at her page. He could tell she was trying not to smile. Maelis continued.

“The Ledger will want follow-up.”

“There it is.”

“Want.”

He looked at her. She tapped the packet.

“Not own.”

That stopped him. Maelis had spent most of their first conversations trying to measure something the System refused to recognize. Somewhere along the way, she had learned the difference between documenting a person and claiming one.

Maybe he had learned a few things too. He did not say that.

“I’m not staying for examinations.”

“I know.”

“You may ask later.”

“I know.”

“No surprise cages.”

Maelis stared at him.

“That was never authorized.”

“Comforting.”

Jessa finally smiled. Maelis ignored her.

“If the Ledger contacts you again, you can answer or refuse.”

That was all he wanted. Maybe not all. Enough.

He left them with the records. They had work after he was gone. That felt important.

Tavian was near the gate reviewing a route marker with two workers. He finished before acknowledging him. He waited.

Growth continued to be unpleasant. When he turned, he looked at the pack over his shoulder.

“So.”

“So.”

“You are leaving.”

“I was hoping to make it more mysterious.”

“You packed food.”

That had damaged the effect. Brynn approached from the militia yard. She carried no reports this time.

Suspicious.

“The emergency restriction is lifted,” she said.

“What restriction?”

“The one that let us keep an unverified person inside Greywake while we determined whether he was a public-safety threat.”

“Oh. That.”

“You were informed.”

“Was I?”

“Yes.”

Probably. He had been busy being undefined. Brynn folded her arms.

“You are free to travel.”

No citizenship. No registration. No System correction.

No official answer to what he was. Just no immediate reason for Greywake to say he had to stay. That mattered more.

Tavian looked toward the road outside the gate.

“I will be north for a while.”

“Broken North?”

“Wider route monitoring.”

“Sounds relaxing.”

“It will not be.”

“Good.”

Red looked at Tavian. There was probably a farewell available somewhere. Neither of them went looking very hard.

“You were reliable when it mattered,” Tavian said.

He had already said something similar. Apparently this was the expanded edition.

“Careful. I might develop a reputation.”

“You already have one.”

That was true. Unfortunately. Brynn said, “Greywake could use you.”

There it was. He looked at her. She continued before he could answer.

“That was not an order.”

Also important.

“Militia?”

“No.”

“Road work?”

“Maybe.”

“Unregistered anomaly kept near the gate in case reality becomes inconvenient?”

“Tempting.”

He liked her. That was part of the problem. Greywake could use him.

Maelis could study him. Sela could treat him. The militia could call him when something needed to survive damage it should not survive.

Workers could ask for impossible tools. Outer communities could ask him to show up when institutions failed them. People could call him hero.

Other people could call him menace. The Ledger could call him anomaly. The System called him nothing.

Every one of those labels came with a shape. Stay long enough, and shapes became expectations. Expectations became duties.

Duties became roles. He had not crossed worlds to become somebody’s permanent emergency equipment. That did not mean Greywake meant nothing.

That was the difficult part. It meant enough that leaving required a decision. The pack was ordinary.

Food. Water. Bandages Sela had forced on him.

A little coin from work already accounted for. A cloak that had survived better than some roads. No legendary weapon.

No sacred map. No glowing arrow pointing toward destiny. He did have directions.

Several. None of them had become a decision. That was how he wanted it.

At the market edge, people noticed the pack. A woman who had thanked him after the council session asked if he was coming back.

“I have no idea.”

A Guild worker he had seen during repairs told him he still owed him for a broken pry bar. He did not remember breaking it. That did not mean he had not.

A militia recruit nodded at him like he was someone important. An older resident watched him like he was something dangerous. One child asked again whether he had learned to make a horse.

“No.”

“Can you make a small horse?”

“Still no.”

He seemed disappointed in his progression. Reasonable. Jessa appeared near the gate with a stack of papers in her arms.

“You are actually going.”

“Apparently.”

“Do you know where?”

“No.”

She looked at him for a second.

“That sounds like you.”

He chose to take it positively. Kellan could not come to the gate. Sela would not leave the treatment house long enough to try.

Maelis was buried in records. Tavian had already returned to work. Brynn was standing ten steps away pretending she was not waiting for him to leave safely.

Nobody dropped their life to follow him.

Good.

They had lives.

That was why any of this had mattered.

He walked through the gate.

Then stopped.

Not for emotion.

A repair wagon was coming in.

The driver pointed at him.

“Move.”

So much for the exit.

He stepped aside while the wagon rolled through carrying timber toward the damaged approaches.

Brynn looked away.

She was definitely hiding a smile.

He waited until the road cleared.

Then he tried again.

No pose this time.

Mostly.

Greywake sat behind him in river fog, hammer noise, damaged roads, new rules, unfinished arguments, and people who had survived each other long enough to start rebuilding.

Somewhere inside, Hesk was still under guard.

Somewhere else, Kellan was probably insulting a repair plan from a chair.

Maelis was writing down facts.

Jessa was copying records.

Sela was threatening patients.

Tavian was watching roads.

Brynn was keeping the whole place from becoming stupid at once.

They would keep going.

So would he.

He did not know what waited beyond Greywake.

That was not a problem.

For once, nobody had assigned him the answer.

He adjusted the pack on his shoulder and walked until the gate disappeared behind the bend.

Then he left Greywake before they could decide what he was.
