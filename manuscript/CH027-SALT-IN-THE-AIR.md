# Chapter 27 — Salt in the Air

**Status:** DRAFT — GATE 9 REVALIDATION  
**Word Count:** 2108  
**Volume:** 2 — Merrowgate Arc

Freedom, I discovered, had expenses.

Not philosophical expenses. Those I could ignore for hours at a time. Actual expenses were more persistent. Food cost money. Beds cost money. Floors sometimes cost money if the owner had correctly identified that travelers became less selective after dark. Crossing a river cost money. Putting my bag on the same boat apparently cost slightly more money, because the bag had achieved independent financial status.

Several days out from Greywake, I had begun to suspect civilization was a conspiracy designed by people who owned chairs. The road had changed gradually after I left the Hollow March behind. Fewer stretches disappeared into forest, more carts moved in both directions, and roadside shelters became actual stopping places with covered yards, feed troughs, cooking fires, and boards full of writing I still could not read.

My literacy remained undefeated.

I had learned a few symbols through repetition. The mark beside a water barrel usually meant something involving water. A painted wheel near a yard generally suggested carts. A large block of text beside a man holding out his hand almost always meant somebody wanted money. That last one had been surprisingly universal.

My pack felt heavier than when I left Greywake even though I had eaten some of what was inside it. The problem was probably my shoulder, or my ribs, or the fact that Sela had somehow managed to fit enough bandages into one wrapped packet to treat a minor military campaign. I had considered leaving them behind until my side started aching after a long morning on the road. Sela did not need the satisfaction of being right from several days away.

The wound itself was closed. Mostly. My body had done what it always did—pulled itself back toward functional with no concern for how unpleasant the process felt. The bruising had faded, but the stiffness had not. Long stretches sitting on a cart made my leg complain when I stood; long stretches walking made it complain that sitting had been an excellent idea.

Regeneration was a miracle if you ignored the customer experience.

I adjusted the strap across my shoulder and joined the line moving toward the river landing. This one was larger than the places I had passed before. Not Greywake large. Greywake had been a settlement trying very hard to be important to the roads around it. This place existed because roads, boats, cargo, and people had already decided it was useful.

Covered wagons waited beside stacked crates. Narrow riverboats bumped against timber landings. Workers rolled barrels down ramps while merchants argued beside scales and chalkboards. I understood none of the writing and maybe one word in twenty of the shouting until I stepped closer to the fare counter and felt the pressure of translated meaning settle over the noise.

A brass Wayfarer Tongue Token hung from a post beside the clerk.

Beautiful little proof that somewhere in this world, somebody had gotten tired of losing money to language barriers.

The clerk looked at me, then at my pack. “Passenger?”

“Yes.”

“South transfer?”

“If that gets me closer to the Brasswater trade routes.”

“It does.”

“Good. How much?”

He told me. I stared at him, then checked the painted board over his shoulder in case literacy had arrived while I was not looking. The symbols remained committed to privacy.

“That includes passage?”

“Yes.”

“And the bag?”

“No.”

Of course it did not. “How much for the bag?”

He told me again. I looked at my bag. The bag offered no financial assistance.

“Does it receive a better seat than I do?”

“No.”

“Food?”

“No.”

“Emotional support?”

The clerk did not blink. “No.”

Professional.

I paid him. He pushed two small stamped pieces across the counter, pointed toward a boarding lane, and immediately looked past me to the next traveler. No questions about where I came from. No magical slate. No sudden revelation that I had broken another piece of administrative reality.

Just money. It was almost refreshing.

I moved away from the counter and counted what remained in my purse by touch before deciding I disliked the answer. I had enough to reach the coast, enough to eat, and enough to avoid sleeping under a cart unless I developed strong feelings about saving money. I did not have enough to continue wandering indefinitely while pretending employment was a problem for future me.

Future me had become annoyingly present.

The boarding area was organized chaos. Cargo went one direction, passengers another, workers somehow moved through both. A crewman with a red cloth tied around one arm checked the stamped piece in my hand, pointed toward a low river vessel, then pointed at my pack. I handed him the second stamp. He nodded, and I had successfully purchased permission for my belongings to continue existing near me.

The vessel sat low in the water beneath stacked cargo secured with ropes and timber braces. The passenger space was not luxurious, unless your definition of luxury included a bench and the possibility of keeping both feet dry. Mine had recently become flexible.

I stepped onto the boarding ramp just as two workers began guiding a loaded handcart toward the cargo section. Compact wooden crates were strapped together beneath canvas. Nothing mysterious. Nothing glowing. Nothing screaming.

A larger vessel passed farther out in the channel. Its wake reached us a few seconds later, and the riverboat shifted under the ramp. One wheel of the handcart slipped sideways on the wet timber. The load tilted toward the edge, and the worker behind it shouted.

Everyone moved at once, but nobody panicked. One crewman dropped his shoulder into the cart frame. Another kicked a wedge loose from beside the ramp. A third grabbed a rope already tied to the side rail.

The Wayfarer Token near the boarding post caught one shouted instruction clearly.

“Hold that line!”

My hand was already halfway toward the cart. I stopped, grabbed the rope instead, and planted one boot against the raised edge of the ramp. The load pulled hard enough to bite the line across my palm. My shoulder objected immediately. So did my side.

“Not yet!” the crewman shouted.

I held. The man at the wheel shoved the wedge under it while another worker reset his grip. The boat rose, dipped, then settled after the wake passed.

“Now. Pull.”

I pulled while they shoved. The cart rolled forward six inches, enough for the wheel to climb back into place. Two workers drove wedges behind it while the man at the front secured another line.

Then it was done. No explosion, no monster, no impossible weapon falling out of my imagination. Just five people preventing several hundred pounds of cargo from going into the river because everyone did one useful thing at the correct time.

My shoulder hurt. I had survived worse.

That was probably not the lesson.

The crewman took the rope from me, checked the cart, then looked at my stance. “Good.”

That was it. He went back to work.

No applause followed. No grateful merchant rushed forward to declare me the mysterious savior of commercial transportation. Nobody asked my name. A worker carrying a barrel jerked his chin toward the passenger area.

“You’re blocking the ramp.”

I moved.

Personal growth continued to be humiliatingly practical.

The boat pushed away from the landing not long after that. The river widened as the day went on, traffic thickening with it. Barges moved under high bundles of timber and sacks. Narrower craft cut between them carrying passengers, baskets, animals, and once a cage containing something feathered that stared at me with personal dislike.

I respected the commitment.

Without a Wayfarer Token nearby, most conversation returned to meaningless sound. I could hear differences now, though. Some speech had the harder rhythm I associated with the people around Greywake. Other voices rose and fell differently. Clothing changed too: brighter wraps, shorter layered coats made for movement, and more jewelry that looked practical enough to become emergency money.

The cargo changed with the people. Grain, iron fittings, dyed cloth, salt-packed barrels, timber, ceramic jars sealed with wax, bundles of rope thicker than my wrist. Greywake had taught me to look at roads as survival. Here, movement itself had become an industry.

By the time I left the river vessel for the next road transfer, the air had changed. At first I thought rain was coming. The wind carried moisture, but not the clean river smell I had grown used to. There was something sharper under it. Mineral. Bitter.

Then a gust came across the road and I tasted salt.

I stopped long enough for the person behind me to nearly walk into my back. He said something I did not understand, stepped around me, and kept going.

Fair.

For a moment, Earth came back in a way that had nothing to do with headlights, rain, or metal screaming. Just coastlines. Wind. That smell that got into clothes and hair and made everything feel slightly damp no matter how clear the sky was.

I had not realized how much I missed knowing what a place smelled like.

A cart wheel splashed muddy water onto my boot.

Veyr remained committed to balance.

The road rose gradually over the next stretch while traffic became heavier. Wagons with painted merchant marks passed in both directions. Porters moved in groups beneath long carrying poles. I saw more Wayfarer Tokens hanging from roadside posts and temporary trade counters, each one surrounded by people making deals faster than I could follow.

Between them, language became noise again. I did not mind as much. There was too much to look at.

The first mast appeared over the rooftops ahead. Then another. Then enough that counting became pointless.

I slowed without meaning to as the land dropped beyond the road and the world opened. Water stretched farther than any river had a right to. Ships sat against it in layers—small coastal craft, heavy cargo vessels, narrow fast-looking hulls, and shapes I did not know enough to classify. Sails hung furled or half-raised. Cranes turned over the waterfront. Warehouses climbed behind the docks in stacked rows of stone, timber, painted doors, hanging signs, and flags.

Chain bridges connected raised sections over channels crowded with smaller boats. Dry docks cut into the edge of the port like enormous open mouths. Workers crossed platforms, crews moved over decks, merchants stood under awnings, and porters threaded between carts with the confidence of people who knew exactly which stranger was about to step into the wrong lane.

I adjusted my coat. The road dust had reduced its authority somewhat, but presentation was a discipline. I straightened my shoulders, ignored the complaint from the left one, and walked toward the outer port traffic with the calm expression of a man arriving exactly where he intended.

Three porters came toward me carrying a long crate between them.

They did not slow.

I moved aside.

A cart driver pointed sharply at the lane I had almost stepped into and shouted something that required no translation. I moved again.

The mysterious arrival was going well.

Near the outer staging area, another Wayfarer Token hung from a tall post painted with several symbols I could not read. A worker stood beside it directing foot traffic.

“Arrivals that way,” he said, pointing toward a set of covered counters farther ahead. “Cargo declarations left. Passenger intake center lane. Keep the freight path clear.”

“Passenger intake,” I repeated.

“Yes.”

I looked past him. More docks disappeared around the curve of the harbor. More ships waited farther out. Beyond the warehouses, towers and elevated walkways broke the skyline. Flags snapped above buildings whose owners were presumably important enough to need flags.

I had no idea who any of them were.

Perfect.

The worker gave me a look. “Are you going?”

“In a moment.”

“You cannot stand here.”

Less perfect.

I stepped away from the freight path and looked at what was left of my money. A large port meant work. It meant merchants who needed things moved, guarded, fixed, found, carried, threatened, or occasionally persuaded to stop being stupid. It meant ships going somewhere else when I got bored. It meant information.

Most importantly, it meant I did not have enough money to develop standards.

I tied the purse shut and started toward the passenger intake lane. No prophecy had called me here. No divine message had arrived. No glowing marker had pointed at the city.

Just salt in the air, money running low, and a place large enough to make both of those problems interesting.

Merrowgate would do.
