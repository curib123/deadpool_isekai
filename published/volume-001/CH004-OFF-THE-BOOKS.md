# Chapter 4 — Off the Books

"No standard injury payment," Rovan Hesk said. "No registered-traveler guarantee. No normal contract appeal through the registry."

Luck looked at the chair the Roadmaster had offered him. "Does sitting down count as acceptance?"

"No."

He sat. Jessa Vale remained beside the desk with the forms. A brass language token stood between them, doing nothing at all for the writing Luck could not read.

Outside the office, a damaged wheel rattled across the Guild yard. Hesk waited for the noise to pass before placing a page on the table. He wore a working coat repaired at one elbow; every person who had passed him in the hall had moved out of his way.

"Private labor paid from discretionary expense," he said. "We record the job, supplies, payment, and completion. We don't create a certified identity you don't have."

"So off the books."

"Off the worker registry. The money stays on the books."

"An efficient distinction. What does it buy me?"

Hesk gave him a number. Luck held his expression until Jessa translated its actual value: several ordinary meals, basic lodging beyond one night, a little left for road supplies if he was careful.

That was money. Not much, but enough to change where he slept.

Hesk pointed to a bridge symbol northeast of Greywake. "Rain damaged the approach. Repair crew leaves at first light. A supply wagon, tools, replacement timber. You carry, hold, and help where the foreman needs you. If animals threaten the crew, you follow the guards' orders."

Luck looked at the short line between the town and bridge. Roads were remarkably harmless when drawn small enough.

"Why me?"

"You're available."

"Nothing about my unusual talents?"

"Do you have a talent for being available tomorrow?"

Luck decided not to encourage him. "How long?"

"Same day if the repair goes well. Shelter and food travel with the crew if it doesn't."

"And the things you opened with? The protections I don't get?"

"Still absent."

Hesk had not smiled once. It would have been easier to distrust a man selling an obviously wonderful opportunity. This one put the bad bargain where Luck could see it and waited for hunger to make the argument.

"I have an injured arm and ankle," Luck said.

"Then tell the foreman what you can do. I need another pair of hands, not someone falling off a brace to save an explanation."

"A weapon?"

"The guards have weapons. Shared tools are for the work."

Luck touched the pocket containing his folded knife. Good enough for string. Not good enough to make that answer pleasant.

Hesk rose. "Jessa will read every term. Refuse if the price isn't worth the risk. I have a yard to run."

He left before Luck could prepare anything suitably final to say. Jessa moved the papers to the public counter, where another token served the waiting workers.

"Complete version," Luck told her.

"I haven't asked yet."

"You were about to."

"That one you really did predict."

He allowed himself a small, dignified smile. Then she started reading, and he listened.

The advance came out of the final payment. Food, water, weather covering, and assigned tools came from the Guild. The arrangement did not grant registered-worker status or change his gate restrictions. It carried none of the standard identity-linked injury or appeal provisions she had described.

When she finished, Luck asked, "What happens if I don't come back?"

Jessa held the top sheet still. Around them, the hall continued working: boots, coins, an argument over a route tag. The question had not stopped anything except her hand.

"The foreman reports what happened. The payment and assignment remain recorded."

"And the protections that follow an ordinary worker's identity?"

"They don't exist for you."

Luck looked at the page he couldn't read. "Read that part again."

She did. No soothing substitute words. He appreciated her more for it and liked the offer less.

Captain Brynn Alder came in from the yard before he answered. Her eyes went from his entrant tag to the form, then to Hesk returning with a rolled map.

"Private labor?" she asked.

"Yes," Hesk said.

"This doesn't change his restrictions."

"I've told him."

"Or make the militia a guarantor."

"I haven't asked you to be one."

Luck looked between them. "I assume someone is eventually going to tell me I'm responsible for myself."

"You are," Brynn said.

"There it is."

She ignored the line. "Do you understand the terms?"

"I understand that being offered work isn't the same as being protected while I do it."

Brynn glanced at Jessa, then back at him. "Good. Keep the entrant tag. The gate will ask for it when you return."

She left without rescuing him from the decision. Hesk waited. Jessa's pen stayed above the page.

The three unfamiliar coins were still in Luck's coat, untouched and no more useful than they had been at the gate. Last night's cook might accept another night of water hauling. That was a choice too. It just wasn't a choice he wanted to keep making.

"I'll take the work," he said. "But read it once more before you mark anything."

Jessa did. Only after his spoken acceptance did she stamp the page and place several silver marks in his hand.

Real money. An advance, technically, but it had weight and could apparently purchase a door that closed.

She gave him a crew card with a bridge symbol and Guild seal. At the supply counter, a clerk issued a weather sheet, a filled waterskin, and wrapped travel food. Jessa explained that the wagon carried a charged speech token; Luck would need to stay within its range to understand instructions.

"No help with writing?" he asked.

"Not from that token."

"Written language continues its campaign against me."

This time she smiled. "First light. Show the foreman the bridge card."

In the yard, a porter nearly caught Luck's bad ankle with a handcart. Luck stepped aside as the cart struck a rut. A loose iron pin bounced from the load and landed beside his boot.

He picked it up. The porter looked from his hand to the cart.

"You heard that coming loose?"

"Your left side was complaining."

The man checked. A crack showed along a wooden brace behind the wheel; he called another worker and pulled the cart out of traffic. Luck returned the pin and left before anyone asked him to distinguish one wheel noise from another.

At the yard gate, he opened his hand and looked at the silver again. Tomorrow he would have food, a crew, and a job that sounded simple on a map.

Behind him, someone told the foreman that the new man had an ear for failing equipment.

Luck closed his hand.

Apparently the job was getting larger already.
