# Story Structure Bible

**Project:** This Isekai Has a Bug. Unfortunately, It's Me.  
**Protagonist:** Luck Everhart  
**Authority:** CURRENT STRUCTURAL SOURCE OF TRUTH  
**Effective:** 2026-10-03  
**POV:** Distant third-person limited centered on Luck Everhart  
**Only supernatural power:** Fortune Distortion

This file governs story construction. If a roadmap, scene plan, QA file, or historical retcon conflicts with it, this file and the current manuscript canon win.

# 1. Series Promise

The series is a cinematic dark-fantasy adventure/comedy about a man who deliberately performs the role of a mysterious mastermind while an invisible probability anomaly keeps making his improvised choices look far more calculated than they are.

The story must deliver all of these at the same time:

1. **Luck is entertaining on purpose.** He likes entrances, pauses, ominous lines, good exits, and the social power of looking as though he knows more than he does.
2. **The luck is real but invisible.** Fortune Distortion bends possible outcomes without creating impossible ones.
3. **Luck does not know the power is his.** He can call an event lucky, notice suspicious timing, and remember theories people tell him, but he never correctly accepts a personal supernatural probability ability.
4. **Other people remain competent.** Their expertise, institutions, motives, and mistakes continue independently of Luck.
5. **Misunderstandings create plot.** A mistaken theory about Luck must alter a decision, relationship, threat, obligation, investigation, or future expectation.
6. **Consequences persist.** Injury, death, money, broken property, evidence, debt, trust, law, rumors, and political outcomes do not reset for comedy.
7. **The world has its own story.** Luck intrudes on Veyr; he is not the registered Hero and does not replace every native plot.

When resolving contradictions, use this order:

1. Series Constitution

Historical documents may explain how the project changed, but they are not allowed to reintroduce removed powers, POVs, names, or character models.

# 3. Luck Everhart Story Engine

Every important Luck scene should use some version of:

**ordinary desire → practical obstacle → theatrical or improvised choice → visible causal chain → unexpected opening → somebody must act → witness interpretation → persistent consequence**

The ordinary desire may be:
- food;
- money;
- shelter;
- freedom;
- access;
- information;
- curiosity;
- reputation;
- entertainment;
- avoiding embarrassment;
- helping a person he currently cares about.

The scene does not need a supernatural coincidence every time. The absence of a lucky event can itself create tension and prevent the pattern from becoming mechanical.

# 4. Fortune Distortion Structure

Fortune Distortion is passive.

It may alter which already-possible outcome happens through:
- footing;
- aim;
- timing;
- mechanical reliability;
- route choice;
- random selection;
- interruptions;
- ordinary arrivals;
- falling/rolling objects;
- existing defects;
- social misreads;
- discovery of already-existing information.

Every major lucky chain must answer:
1. What variables already existed?
2. Which variable changed?
3. Why was that change possible?
4. What opening did it create?
5. Who still had to act?
6. What did the outcome cost or complicate?

Never use luck as a substitute for:
- investigation;
- expert knowledge;
- emotional decisions;
- political consequences;
- ordinary competence;
- a needed sacrifice or cost.

# 5. Scene Design

Every scene has:

- **POV goal:** what Luck wants now;
- **other-character goal:** what at least one other participant wants;
- **obstacle:** what prevents both from getting a clean outcome;
- **staging:** people, exits, hazards, tools, distances, and important objects established before use;
- **attempt:** a choice that changes the situation;
- **turn:** information, luck, failure, opposition, or consequence changes the scene;
- **exit condition:** the scene ends because the situation is materially different.

Delete, merge, or rebuild scenes that only repeat information.

# 6. Chapter Design

Every chapter must contain:

- a concrete immediate objective or question;
- escalation or complication;
- at least one character-specific decision;
- a meaningful change;
- continuity with prior costs;
- a reason the next chapter must exist.

A chapter may be action, investigation, travel, politics, comedy, aftermath, or relationship pressure. It may not exist only to explain lore.

Recommended chapter rhythm:

**hook → orientation → pressure → choice → turn → consequence → forward pull**

# 7. Misunderstanding Design

Do not use the same joke repeatedly.

Valid forms include:
- accident interpreted as strategy;
- hesitation interpreted as confidence;
- failed performance interpreted as intimidation;
- a vague line later appears prophetic;
- an ordinary retreat looks like a deliberate feint;
- an ally correctly exploits an opening and attributes the setup to Luck;
- an enemy overreacts to reputation and causes the failure;
- a rumor helps Luck immediately and hurts him later;
- Luck deliberately tries to look impressive and nobody cares.

Witnesses must base theories on real observations. They can be wrong about motive without being stupid about evidence.

# 8. Reputation Progression

Reputation escalates by **audience size and consequence**, not by power level.

Use five broad states:

1. **Unknown** — one or two people notice odd survival.
2. **Suspiciously capable** — workers/guards believe he sees more than he says.
3. **Locally notorious** — conflicting stories circulate inside one destination.
4. **Reputation precedes him** — later destinations receive distorted versions before he arrives.
5. **Competing legends** — factions deliberately weaponize incompatible stories about him.

Luck may become better at using reputation socially. He never becomes better at consciously controlling Fortune Distortion.

# 9. Character Independence Rule

Every major supporting character must have:
- an objective that exists without Luck;
- competence Luck does not replace;
- a limit or flaw;
- information Luck needs or cannot produce;
- at least one decision caused by their own values rather than Luck's aura.

No character exists only to praise, explain, or be rescued by Luck.

# 10. Conflict Design

The strongest conflicts are not solved by killing one opponent.

Use layered objectives such as:
- protect people and preserve evidence;
- win a fight without destroying infrastructure;
- expose wrongdoing without collapsing a system people depend on;
- escape while keeping an ally from taking the blame;
- reach a location while injured;
- secure legal access without a System identity;
- stop sabotage while identifying who benefits.

Luck can survive while still losing the real objective.

# 11. Injury and Resource Continuity

Luck is mortal.

Track:
- wounds;
- treatment;
- recovery time;
- exhaustion;
- money;
- food;
- lodging;
- transport;
- equipment;
- clothing damage;
- evidence;
- favors;
- promises;
- legal status.

Fortune Distortion may reduce or redirect a hit before it lands. It does not erase damage afterward.

# 12. Mystery Structure

Mystery proceeds through:
- observable contradiction;
- partial explanation;
- competing interpretation;
- bounded evidence;
- delayed writer truth.

Do not use mystery to conceal basic scene logic.

Luck may learn more facts while remaining wrong about the supernatural source of his luck.

# 13. Arc Structure

One major volume = one destination arc.

Each destination needs:
1. arrival with practical pressure;
2. local identity and culture;
3. local problem independent of Luck;
4. Luck's selfish/personal reason to engage;
5. early competence test;
6. evidence that the surface explanation is incomplete;
7. midpoint pattern;
8. Luck-caused or Luck-enabled complication;
9. consequence phase;
10. coalition or opposing structure;
11. climax where multiple characters own essential functions;
12. local resolution;
13. departure with carried consequences.

The local antagonist must have a coherent goal that would exist if Luck never arrived.

# 14. Volume 1 — Greywake Structural Function

Greywake introduces:
- Luck's chosen identity;
- the UNDEFINED / NO RECORD anomaly;
- frontier economics;
- the difference between the protected main road and expendable outer routes;
- Tavian's ecological expertise;
- Maelis's evidence discipline;
- Hesk's sacrifice logic;
- the first major public construction of Luck's false mastermind reputation.

Greywake's central question:

**Who gets counted when survival becomes logistics?**

Luck's involvement begins because he needs money and freedom, not because he decides to save the settlement.

His major mistake is acting against the lure network before the whole migration geometry is understood, contributing to backflow. That consequence prevents the arc from becoming a consequence-free genius fantasy.

The climax requires workers, militia, route knowledge, records, engineering, evacuation, and Luck-created openings.

# 15. Volume 2 — Merrowgate Structural Function

Merrowgate expands the story from frontier logistics to commercial systems and reputation.

Its local problem:
engineered port instability is being converted into insurance, debt, security, and political leverage for centralized private control.

Merrowgate's central question:

**Who owns a free port when control can be purchased through fear?**

Luck arrives needing money, passage, and a practical identity workaround.

His major mistake:
he accepts a deliberately off-manifest cargo job because being outside normal verification is profitable and flattering. He does not know the cargo supports later sabotage, but he knowingly chooses not to know enough.

Volume 2 must not use conjured structures, Play Logic, regeneration, or deliberate probability control. Rescue and climax solutions use:
- workers;
- ropes;
- cranes;
- brakes;
- counterweights;
- access routes;
- Harbor Watch authority;
- shipwright expertise;
- documents;
- timing;
- existing mechanical failures;
- Fortune Distortion creating openings.

A lucky chain can save a person or preserve evidence. It cannot rebuild a harbor by itself.

# 16. Comedy / Seriousness Balance

Comedy may exist:
- before danger;
- during low-stakes friction;
- in Luck's private reaction;
- after a crisis once human cost has been acknowledged.

Do not place a punchline directly over:
- death;
- grief;
- severe injury;
- betrayal;
- irreversible loss.

When Luck becomes serious, he remains Luck. He does not become a different solemn character; he simply stops performing for entertainment and focuses on the immediate problem.

# 17. Endgame Lock

The final series state must preserve the central irony:

Other people may have sophisticated theories about Luck.

The reader may have overwhelming evidence of Fortune Distortion.

Luck himself still interprets the pattern through coincidence, timing, ordinary causes, other people's skill, and the success of his own theatrical performance.

No final power tutorial.
No conscious luck activation.
No second awakening.
No chosen-Hero conversion.

# 18. Manuscript QA

A chapter fails if:
- the spatial logic is unclear during action;
- a needed object appears without setup;
- Luck solves a knowledge problem through luck instead of evidence;
- supporting characters become incompetent to make him impressive;
- the same misunderstanding joke repeats without a new consequence;
- Luck consciously relies on supernatural luck;
- injuries or costs disappear;
- dialogue exists only to explain the plot;
- the chapter ends where it began;
- the ending has no forward pressure;
- a historical removed power returns.

A chapter passes when the reader can answer:
- What did Luck want?
- What stopped him?
- What changed?
- What did it cost?
- What did someone misunderstand?
- What consequence now survives into the next chapter?
